Working through a decision
Do we take the Kelowna contract?
Doubles payroll for eleven weeks. Best month you'd ever bill. Cash gets tight in week six.
A decision this size gets argued three different ways, and the three don't agree. Here they are separately, so you can see which one you're actually weighing.
THE MONEY CASE
Take it. Gross margin holds at 31%, and eleven weeks at that volume clears the van loan a year early. The week-six dip is survivable with a deposit from Hensley or one month's delay on the truck.
Weakest point: it assumes materials land where you quoted them. You haven't got the second supplier quote back.
THE PEOPLE CASE
Be careful. Eleven weeks of doubled load falls on fourteen people, and two of them carried the Vernon job in April without a break. If anyone walks in September you don't just lose the contract, you lose the crew that could have delivered it.
Weakest point: you may know something about your crew's appetite that isn't in any of your records.
THE PACE CASE
Don't take it as it stands. You're at 62 hours in a normal week. Eleven weeks like this puts you well past what you said you'd hold, straight through the autumn. Taking it means deciding now what gets protected — not hoping it survives.
Weakest point: seasons of hard work are sometimes right. This argument can't tell the difference on its own.
Where I land, for what it's worth
Get the supplier quote and ask Hensley for the deposit this week. Both answers arrive before you have to commit, and both are cheap to ask for.
If the numbers hold, the money case wins and the pace case becomes a scheduling problem rather than a reason to decline. If they don't, you've saved yourself an argument you didn't need to have.
